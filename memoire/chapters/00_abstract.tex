\begin{abstract}
\noindent Does the yield advantage of irrigated maize grow with extreme summer heat? I study this question for grain maize in France, using department-level yields that the national agricultural statistics report \emph{separately} for irrigated and rainfed maize (2000--2025), daily SAFRAN--ISBA reanalysis weather at 8~km, and heat exposure measured on the parcels where maize is grown. The outcome is the yield gap between the two modes in the same department and year: shocks that hit both modes proportionally cancel out, and stable differences such as soils are absorbed by department effects. The specification, the hypothesis and the decision rule were fixed in a document whose SHA-256 hash was recorded in the project's decision log, now published with the code but dated by the author alone, before any estimation on 2000--2009; exploratory results for 2010--2025 were already known. In 17 departments (\QoneN{} department-years), each degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C in July--August is associated with a \QonePct\,\% wider yield gap in favour of irrigated maize (one-sided wild cluster bootstrap by year, $p = \QoneP$); in placebo simulations on the same data this test rejects a true null \QoneSizeWCR\,\% of the time at the 5\,\% level. Each degree-day is associated with about \QoneRainfedPct\,\% lower rainfed yields and \QoneIrrigatedPct\,\% lower irrigated yields. The estimate keeps its sign on 2000--2009 with the maize mask, at about half the 2010--2025 estimate with the same mask, and is unchanged with a 2008 mask of parcel blocks; but it is not significant when heat is averaged over the whole department, and the replication does not hold with that measure. I propose, after the fact, that department-wide averages understate the heat experienced by maize in departments with uplands; why that measure gives a stronger association over 2010--2025 remains unexplained. The result is an association, not the causal effect of irrigation. A second, methodological finding is more general: in these panels, standard errors clustered by department or Conley (200~km) reject a true null in \UsualSizeMin\,\% to \UsualSizeMax\,\% of cases. With them, heat would have appeared ``significantly'' associated with lower wheat yields ($p = \WheatPdep$), a result that a correctly sized test does not support ($p = \WheatPwcr$). Wheat yields over 1959--2025 and out-of-sample yield forecasts with weather and satellite NDVI give null results, which are reported in full.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} heat stress; irrigation; maize; France; panel data; wild cluster bootstrap; pre-specification.\\
\textbf{JEL codes:} Q15, Q54, C23, C12.
\end{abstract}
