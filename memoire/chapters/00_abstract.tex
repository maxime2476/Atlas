\begin{abstract}
\noindent Does the yield advantage of irrigated maize grow with extreme summer heat? I study this question for grain maize in France, using department-level yields that the national agricultural statistics report \emph{separately} for irrigated and rainfed maize (2000--2025), daily SAFRAN--ISBA reanalysis weather at 8~km, and heat exposure measured on the parcels where maize is grown. The outcome is the yield gap between the two modes in the same department and year. The specification and its decision rule were written after an exploratory analysis of 2010--2025 and before any estimate on 2000--2009; they entered the public repository together with the results, so this order rests on the author's decision log. In 17 departments (\QoneN{} department-years), which exclude the South-West, each degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C in July--August is associated with a \QonePct\,\% wider yield gap in favour of irrigated maize (one-sided wild cluster bootstrap by year, $p = \QoneP$; the test rejects a true null \QoneSizeWCR\,\% and \PowerCheckSize\,\% of the time in two placebo runs). This meets the pre-specified rule, but \ExploSharePct\,\% of the sample comes from the exploratory years. The only new evidence, 2000--2009, keeps the sign at about half the 2010--2025 estimate, a difference driven by 2003 ($p = \QoneReplP$, with a power of about \ReplPower\,\%), and does not hold when heat is averaged over the whole department. The 90\,\% interval, from inverting the bootstrap, runs from \CiNinetyLow{} to \CiNinetyHigh\,\% per degree-day. Dropping any single department leaves the estimate significant, but without the six departments with uplands, where the heat measure matters most, it falls to \NoReliefPct\,\% and is not significant ($p = \NoReliefP$), although its slope does not differ detectably from theirs; a more conservative cluster jackknife gives $p = \CvThreeP$. The result holds with E-OBS temperatures and with a climatic water balance instead of soil moisture. A pre-specified interaction with crisis-level water restrictions has the opposite sign to the hypothesis. The result is an association, not the causal effect of irrigation. A methodological check matters for these panels: standard errors clustered by department, or Conley (uniform kernel, 200~km), reject a true null in \UsualSizeMin\,\% to \UsualSizeMax\,\% of placebo simulations. With them, heat would have appeared ``significantly'' associated with lower wheat yields ($p = \WheatPdep$), a result that a correctly sized test does not support ($p = \WheatPwcr$). Wheat yields over 1959--2025 and out-of-sample forecasts with weather and satellite NDVI give null results, which are reported in full.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} heat stress; irrigation; maize; France; panel data; wild cluster bootstrap; pre-specification.\\
\textbf{JEL codes:} Q15, Q54, C23, C12.
\end{abstract}
