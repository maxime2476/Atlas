\section{Results: heat and the irrigated--rainfed maize gap}
\label{sec:maize}

This section reports the main result, then asks four questions about it: how strong the statistical evidence is, whether it holds on years never used, whether it depends on how heat is measured, and whether it is driven by particular departments or by a change in what is harvested. All tests are those fixed in the specification unless they are marked \emph{added later}. Each added check was written in the decision log, with its reading rule, before it was computed; \cref{app:deviations} gives its timing, and none of them changes the status of a pre-specified hypothesis.

\subsection{The data at a glance}

In the main sample (\MaizeMainDeps{} departments, \MaizeMainPairs{} department-years), irrigated grain maize yields \YieldIrr{} quintals per hectare on average, against \YieldRain{} for rainfed maize. The median gap is \GapMedianPct\,\%, and the irrigated yield is above the rainfed one in all but \GapNotAboveCount{} of the \MaizeMainPairs{} department-years (it is lower in \GapBelowCount). The question is not whether irrigated maize yields more, but how this advantage moves with summer heat.

\paragraph{H1 is established under the pre-specified rule.} Each degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C in July--August is associated with a \QonePct\,\% wider yield gap in favour of irrigated maize ($p = \QoneP$). The measured size of this test on the same data is \QoneSizeWCR\,\%, below the 10\,\% limit. ``Established'' is meant in the sense of this rule only. \ExploPairs{} of the \MaizeMainPairs{} department-years (\ExploSharePct\,\%) belong to 2010--2025, the period whose exploratory analysis suggested the hypothesis; the only new evidence is the decade 2000--2009 (\cref{subsec:maize-repl}).

\paragraph{How precise is the estimate?} Inverting the bootstrap (added later) gives a 90\,\% interval for $\beta$ of \CiNinetyLow{} to \CiNinetyHigh\,\% per degree-day (95\,\%: \CiNinetyFiveLow{} to \CiNinetyFiveHigh\,\%). The sign is supported; the magnitude is known only to within a factor of eight. With the same placebo as the size check, the test detects a true effect of the estimated size (0.016) in \PowerFull\,\% of cases and half of it in \PowerHalf\,\% (added later; size under the null in this run: \PowerCheckSize\,\%).

\paragraph{Both modes lose, rainfed maize much more.} Each degree-day is associated with about \QoneRainfedPct\,\% lower rainfed yields and about \QoneIrrigatedPct\,\% lower irrigated yields; both are significant after the Holm correction, and the size of these two tests, measured later with the same placebo as H1, is \HtwobSize\,\% and \HtwocSize\,\%. Rainfed maize is about \QoneRatio{} times more sensitive to heat. Irrigation is therefore associated with a smaller sensitivity, not with its disappearance. Wetter soils are associated with a narrower gap ($\hat\delta = \QoneSwi$ per unit of the soil wetness index), as expected if the advantage of irrigated maize comes from water.

\paragraph{Water restrictions (H3).} The slope of the gap on heat is not smaller where crisis-level water restrictions lasted longer; the interaction even has the opposite sign ($p_{\text{Holm}} = \QoneHthreeP$). This result is not interpretable causally. Restrictions are decided during the driest summers and in the driest departments, so their duration is tied to the very conditions that drive the gap. Which mechanism produces the positive interaction is a question I did not test. Possible ones, none checked: restrictions are decided where the gap is already widening; the indicator counts days with at least one zone of the department at crisis level, which does not say whether irrigated maize was restricted; and orders may exempt some uses. The indicator is also coarse, and the sample has only 10 summers. For water policy, this is the most directly relevant result of the monograph, and it does not show that restrictions reduced the advantage of irrigated maize.

\subsection{How strong is the statistical evidence?}
\label{subsec:maize-inference}

\Cref{sec:inference} showed why the bootstrap by year is the reference test on these panels. Its validity, however, rests on the clusters, and one cluster dominates: in the weighted model, 2003 alone carries \LevThree\,\% of the residual variation of heat ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \NeffLevAll$; the share of \NeffMaizeShareTwoThousandThree\,\% in \cref{sec:exposure} is computed without weights or soil moisture). With so much leverage in one year, it is worth asking whether other valid procedures agree. \Cref{tab:maize-inference} reports three, all added later.

The procedures do not agree on the strength of the evidence. Randomisation over permuted years gives a smaller $p$-value, but its null, like the placebo's, leaves the true effect of heat in the error. The cluster jackknife, which \textcite{mackinnon2023} recommend when a few clusters carry much of the leverage, gives $p = \CvThreeP$: with it, H1 would not be significant at 5\,\%. The decision rule of phase 4 bis rests on the bootstrap and is unchanged, but the result is not significant under every reasonable inference. Two further diagnostics support the bootstrap itself: a placebo that keeps each summer's heat in its own year and shuffles it across departments gives a size of \WithinYearSize\,\%, and the usual placebo shows no abnormal rejection rate according to the year that receives the field of 2003. In the same placebo runs, \texttt{fixest} had to correct the Conley matrix in \ConleyFixedPct\,\% of repetitions, which is one more reason not to rely on it. Dropping one year at a time, finally, leaves the estimate between \LoyoYearMinPct{} and \LoyoYearMaxPct\,\% per degree-day and significant in \LoyoYearSig{} cases out of \LoyoYearN; the largest change comes from dropping 2003, which raises it.

\subsection{Replication on years never used}
\label{subsec:maize-repl}

The exploratory analysis that motivated this question had used 2010--2025. On 2000--2009 alone, a decade that no earlier analysis had touched, the estimate keeps its sign: \QoneReplPct\,\% per degree-day, with a one-sided $p = \QoneReplP$. The same mask gives \QoneRecentPct\,\% on 2010--2025, so the estimate for the earlier decade is about half as large. On 2000--2009 without 2003, the point estimate is larger than on 2010--2025 (\cref{tab:maize-measure}): the smaller estimate over the decade comes from 2003, which raises a question about linearity, taken up below.

Four cautions apply. The two-sided $p$-value is \QoneReplPtwo. The test rests on 10 years: measured later with the same placebo, the size of the bootstrap with 10 clusters is \ReplSize\,\%, but its power against a true effect of the estimated size is only \ReplPower\,\%, so a $p$-value near 0.05 is weak evidence. Within the decade, 2003 carries \LevThreeEarly\,\% of the residual variation of heat ($N_{\mathrm{eff}} = \NeffLevEarly$), so the replication is essentially a test on one summer. And, as shown below, the replication holds with the maize mask but not with department-wide weather. I read it as consistent in sign, not as a confirmation of magnitude. An interaction between heat and the decade 2000--2009 (added later) finds no detectable difference between the two slopes ($\DecDiff$, two-sided $p = \DecDiffP$; without 2003, $\DecDiffNoThree$, $p = \DecDiffNoThreeP$): the data cannot say whether the earlier decade is really less sensitive.

\paragraph{Another crop: silage maize.} A different test of the same idea, added later, uses a crop the hypothesis was never estimated on. The SAA also reports, for 2010--2025, the yields of silage maize on irrigated and on rainfed land. Applying the population rule of grain maize gives \FodderDeps{} departments (only five of them with quality status~1, so all are kept and the data are weaker), \FodderN{} department-years and a median gap of \FodderGap\,\%. With heat measured on the silage-maize parcels of the RPG and the same model, each degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C is associated with a gap wider by \FodderPct\,\% (one-sided $p = \FodderP$; measured size \FodderSize\,\%), almost exactly the grain-maize estimate. Under the rule declared before this check, the association replicates on silage maize. It is not an independent sample of summers, since the years overlap with those of grain maize, but the crop, its harvest, its statistics and its mask are different. With the five departments of status~1 only, the estimate is \FodderSOnePct\,\% ($p = \FodderSOneP$, five clusters of departments).

\paragraph{Soil moisture and other weather controls.} Three checks, added later, ask whether the result depends on how the dryness of the summer is controlled for. Without the soil wetness index, the estimate is \NoSwiPct\,\% ($p = \NoSwiP$); with the July--August climatic water balance (precipitation minus evapotranspiration) in its place, \PetPct\,\% ($p = \PetP$). Under the declared rule, the association does not depend on the moisture control chosen. A partially linear model estimated by double machine learning \parencite{chernozhukov2018}, in which soil moisture, precipitation, evapotranspiration and growing degree-days enter through random forests with folds by blocks of years, gives $\hat\beta = \DmlBeta$ (one-sided $p = \DmlP$): flexible controls leave the estimate almost unchanged. None of these models separates heat from drought, however: within departments, degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C and the water balance are correlated at $\CorrPet$ (\cref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Heat at the wrong time.} If the association reflected a persistent difference between departments rather than the summer of the harvest, heat measured at other times would show it too (added later). The heat of the following summer, which cannot affect the harvest, is not associated with the gap (two-sided $p = \LeadP$), and the main coefficient is unchanged when it is added (\LeadMainPct\,\%). The heat of the previous summer is not associated with it either ($p = \LagP$). Within the season, the July--August coefficient stays significant when June and September heat enter the same model (\JaCalPct\,\%, $p = \JaCalP$). June heat has a large but imprecise coefficient (\JunePct\,\% per degree-day, $p = \JuneP$): June rarely exceeds 32\,$^{\circ}$C (\JuneMean{} degree-days on average, against \JaMean{} in July--August), and its few hot Junes are also hot summers. September is no test at all: it almost never exceeds 32\,$^{\circ}$C in these departments (\SeptMean{} degree-days on average).

\subsection{Does the result depend on how heat is measured?}
\label{subsec:maize-measure}

Yes, and this is the main limitation of the result. \Cref{tab:maize-measure} shows where the two measures diverge.

The divergence is concentrated in 2000--2009 ($\MaskEarly$ with the maize mask, $\AreaEarly$ with department area) and persists in that decade even without 2003. On 2010--2025, department-wide weather gives a \emph{larger} coefficient (\AreaRecent{} against \MaskRecent).

What we know about the two measures is the following.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The age of the mask is not the cause.} A mask drawn from 2008 blocks gives almost the same exposure as the 2015--2017 mask and the same estimate.
  \item \textbf{The temperature source is not the cause.} Recomputed from E-OBS, a grid interpolated from station records \parencite{cornes2018}, with the same double-sine method, window and maize mask (added later), heat is correlated at \EobsCorr{} with the SAFRAN measure and gives \EobsPct\,\% per degree-day ($p = \EobsP$). E-OBS counts more degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C, so the coefficients per degree-day are not directly comparable: one within-department standard deviation of heat goes with a gap wider by \EobsSdEffect\,\% with E-OBS and \SimSdEffect\,\% with SIM. The check is partial: some French stations behind E-OBS also feed SAFRAN, only the main model was re-estimated, and the soil wetness control still comes from SIM.
  \item \textbf{Satellite surface temperature points the same way.} The mean daytime land-surface temperature of July--August over the maize mask, from MODIS composites \parencite{wan2021} (added later; protocol fixed before the data were received), is correlated at \LstCorr{} with the degree-days within departments. Used in place of them, each additional degree Celsius of surface temperature goes with a gap wider by \LstPct\,\% ($p = \LstP$). The satellite measure is taken around 10:30, not at the daily maximum, and it mixes irrigated and rainfed fields within each 1~km pixel.
  \item \textbf{The department-area measure understates the 2003 heat where maize grows in departments with uplands.} In \ReliefCount{} such departments, maize fields lie \ReliefAltDiffMin{} to \ReliefAltDiffMax{}~m below the department's average altitude, and department-wide weather understates their 2003 heat by up to \ReliefGapMax{} degree-days (\cref{tab:relief}). This is shown for 2003 only, while the divergence persists on 2000--2009 without 2003. An error that scaled heat by the same factor every year would rescale $\beta$, not remove it; removing the association requires errors that reorder departments from one year to the next, which is not shown.
  \item \textbf{Neither measure is checked against where each mode grows.} Both masks assign the same heat to irrigated and rainfed maize. In departments with uplands, irrigated maize may sit lower in the valleys than rainfed maize, so the mask may describe the heat of irrigated fields better than that of rainfed ones.
  \item \textbf{The maize mask was the measure designated in advance} as the main one, before any estimate on 2000--2009 was computed.
  \item \textbf{The choice did not favour the result already known.} When the specification was written, the only known estimate (phase 6, 2010--2025) used department-wide weather and was significant. On those same years the maize mask gives a smaller estimate (\MaskRecent{} against \AreaRecent).
\end{itemize}
These points favour the maize mask. They do not explain the whole divergence, nor why department-wide weather gives a stronger association over 2010--2025, and the altitude pattern was sought after the results were known.

\paragraph{How much does measurement error attenuate the estimate?} Errors in measured heat that are unrelated to the gap bias $\beta$ towards zero. With two independent measures of the same heat, one can instrument the other and remove that bias (added later). Instrumented by E-OBS, which is strongly related to the SAFRAN measure (first-stage $F = \IvEobsF$, clusters by year), the coefficient becomes \IvEobsBeta, \IvEobsRatio{} times the least-squares estimate; the reduced form, the gap on E-OBS heat, is significant under the bootstrap ($p = \IvEobsRfP$). Because the two grids share some stations, their errors are correlated and this correction is, if anything, too small. Instrumented by satellite surface temperature ($F = \IvLstF$), the ratio reaches \IvLstRatio, but surface temperature may affect the gap through the cooling of irrigated fields and not only through air heat, so that exclusion is doubtful. The magnitudes reported in this section are therefore more likely too small than too large, within the wide interval of \cref{subsec:maize-main}.

\paragraph{The departments with uplands.} Because the measure matters most in these six departments, three checks, added later, ask how much the result rests on them. Dropping one department at a time leaves the estimate between \LooMinPct{} and \LooMaxPct\,\% per degree-day, significant in \LooSig{} cases out of 17. Dropping the six departments with uplands together gives \NoReliefPct\,\% with $p = \NoReliefP$ on the remaining 11: under the rule declared before this check, H1 depends on these departments. But their slope does not differ detectably from that of the other departments (interaction $+\RelInter$, two-sided $p = \RelInterP$), so the loss of significance is consistent with the remaining 11 departments simply carrying less information. Either way, the significance of the result comes from the very departments where the choice of heat measure matters, so the measure and the sample cannot be separated. The honest summary is that the result holds when heat is measured where maize is grown, and that it is sensitive to that choice in the earlier decade and rests on the departments with uplands.

\subsection{Heat, dry air or dry soil?}
\label{subsec:maize-vpd}

Hot French summers are also dry ones, and the literature suggests that much of the damage attributed to heat runs through water: the evaporative demand of hot, dry air \parencite{lobell2013} and the combination of heat with dry soils \parencite{haqiqi2021,lesk2022}. Two checks, added later, look at these channels.

\paragraph{Vapour pressure deficit.} From the daily SIM2 temperatures and relative humidity, the vapour pressure deficit of the afternoon (saturation pressure at the daily maximum minus actual vapour pressure) is averaged over July--August on the maize mask. Used in place of degree-days, it is strongly associated with the gap: \VpdPct\,\% per kPa (one-sided $p = \VpdP$), or \VpdSdEffect\,\% for one within-department standard deviation (\VpdSd~kPa), against \SimSdEffect\,\% for degree-days. When both enter the same model, the vapour pressure deficit keeps its coefficient (\VpdJointPct\,\% per kPa, two-sided $p = \VpdJointP$) and the degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C lose theirs ($\VpdJointHeatPct$\,\%, $p = \VpdJointHeatP$). The two are correlated at \VpdCorr{} within departments, so under the rule declared before this check they cannot be separated with these data. But the pattern is the one \textcite{lobell2013} describe: what this monograph measures as extreme heat behaves like the drying power of hot air. The pattern is not confirmed on silage maize: there the vapour pressure deficit alone is associated with the gap (one-sided $p = \FodderVpdP$), but in the joint model neither it nor the degree-days remains significant ($p = \FodderVpdJointP$ and $\FodderHeatJointP$), so the reading rests on grain maize alone.

\paragraph{Heat on dry soils.} Adding an interaction between degree-days and soil wetness gives the expected sign, a larger effect of heat when soils are drier, but it is not detectable (interaction $\CompoundInter$, two-sided $p = \CompoundP$). Hot summers are already dry ones in this sample, which leaves little variation to tell the two apart.

\paragraph{Do farmers pump more in hot summers?} The mechanism behind the gap is that irrigated fields receive water that rainfed fields do not. Volumes declared for irrigation in the national water withdrawal database (BNPE), summed by department for 2012--2023, allow a check of the first link. With the same fixed effects and weights, they rise with soil dryness (coefficient $\VolSwi$ on the soil wetness index, two-sided $p = \VolSwiP$, \VolN{} department-years) but not detectably with degree-days above 32\,$^{\circ}$C (\VolHeatPct\,\% per degree-day, one-sided $p = \VolHeatP$; $p = \VolVpdP$ for the vapour pressure deficit). The volumes cover all irrigated crops, and in hot summers some withdrawals are restricted, which may cap the response. The check therefore says that water use follows dry soils; it does not say that the gap is caused by the extra water.

\subsection{Is the slope the same everywhere?}
\label{subsec:maize-heterogeneity}

If irrigation protects against heat, the gap might widen more where more maize is irrigated, where the irrigated land differs most from the rest, or where rainfed maize runs out of soil water sooner. Four approaches, all added later, look for such differences; none finds a robust one. The interaction of heat with the irrigated share of grain maize is not detectable (two-sided $p = \IrrInterP$). Nor is its interaction with the available water capacity of the soils under maize, averaged from a 90~m soil map \parencite{romandobarco2019} over the maize parcels of the RPG: it has the expected negative sign, but $p = \RuInterP$ (\RuInterBothP{} with the irrigated share in the same model, with which it correlates at $\RuCorIrr$). The 17 departments span only \RuMin--\RuMax~mm, which leaves little to work with. In the variant with the vapour pressure deficit, the interaction is detectable ($p = \RuVpdP$): the slope of the vapour pressure deficit falls from about \RuVpdLow\,\% per kPa on the shallowest soils to \RuVpdHigh\,\% on the deepest. This is one of five interaction tests on 17 values of the soil variable, so I report it as a lead to confirm, not as a finding. Department-specific slopes, shrunk towards their mean with an empirical Bayes estimator \parencite{dersimonian1986}, show little dispersion ($\tau = \EbTau$; homogeneity test $p = \EbQp$, which ignores the dependence between departments within a year and is therefore, if anything, too permissive): all shrunk slopes lie between \EbMin{} and \EbMax{}, and they correlate at $\EbCorIrr$ with the irrigated share and $+\EbCorAlt$ with the altitude gap, on 17 points. A causal forest \parencite{athey2019} is uninformative with 435 observations: its calibration test detects no heterogeneity ($p = \ForestP$) and its average effect is very imprecise. With 17 departments, the data cannot say where the association is stronger.

\subsection{Is the composition of land moving with heat?}
\label{subsec:maize-composition}

If hot years changed which land is irrigated, or which maize is harvested as grain, the gap could move for reasons unrelated to irrigation itself. Two descriptive checks, not pre-specified, look at the areas reported by the SAA. First, one more degree-day above 32\,$^{\circ}$C is associated with a change of $\CompEst$ in the irrigated share of grain maize area ($p = \CompP$, clustered by year, for information only). This check has little power by construction: areas are largely decided at sowing, before the summer heat. Second, and closer to the more plausible threat, rainfed maize sown for grain may be harvested as silage in dry summers. Over 2010--2025, the only years for which the SAA reports silage maize separately by irrigation mode, the share of rainfed maize harvested as silage (\SilageShare\,\% on average) does not rise detectably in hotter summers (one-sided $p = \SilageP$; added later). This check misses 2003, and silage areas may be recorded before the harvest decision is final. Abandoned fields, finally, stay in the statistics with a zero yield. The absence of a detected response is not evidence that composition is stable.

\subsection{What it is worth}

\Cref{tab:valuation} turns the main estimate into quantities for three hot summers. For each department, the extra production associated with irrigation \emph{in a summer hotter than the department's normal} is
\[
\text{irrigated area} \times \text{rainfed yield} \times \big[e^{\hat\beta\,(\ddthirtytwo - \text{department mean})} - 1\big],
\]
valued at the maize price of the year in current euros. Applying the factor to the rainfed yield is the cautious choice; applied to the irrigated yield, the 2003 figure would be about \ValAltPct\,\% higher. The formula extrapolates the linear estimate to 2003, far above the other summers. A quadratic term in degree-days, added later, has the sign of a slope that flattens at high heat but is not detectable (two-sided $p = \QuadSqP$), and it barely changes the predicted gap of 2003 (\QuadPredThree{} against \LinPredThree{} for the linear model and \ObsThree{} observed); degree-days split into 30--32, 32--34 and above 34\,$^{\circ}$C are too imprecise to show where the response steepens (largest, for 32--34\,$^{\circ}$C: \BinMidPct\,\% per degree-day, $p = \BinMidP$). It also applies to each summer's whole excess heat, national part included, a slope that is identified from differences between departments within a year; in the regression, the national part of each summer is absorbed by the year effects. A simple check suggests it overstates the 2003 figure by about \ValOverPct\,\%: in 2003, the observed gap exceeded each department's average gap by \GapObsTwoThousandThree{} in log terms on average, against \GapPredTwoThousandThree{} predicted by $\hat\beta$.

In the 17 departments, the extra production associated with irrigation in the summer of 2003 is worth about \QoneValTwoThousandThree{} million euros (indicative range \QoneValLow{} to \QoneValHigh). In 2022, fewer quintals are worth more than in 2019 because the price of maize was about twice as high. These figures are gross production values in current euros. They are not the total value of irrigation, which also includes its average advantage; not a value net of the cost of water, energy and labour; not a value per cubic metre or the cost of a water restriction; and not a causal gain. They also leave out the part of the advantage associated with drier soils, since $\beta$ is measured at a given soil wetness.

\paragraph{A value per cubic metre.} A rough value per cubic metre is possible where grain maize dominates irrigation. In six departments of the main sample (01, 03, 18, 63, 67, 68), grain maize occupied more than half of the irrigated area in the 2020 agricultural census. There I attribute to maize the BNPE volume times that share and divide by the SAA irrigated maize area. The resulting dose has a median of \WatDose~m$^3$/ha over 2012--2023, close to the 1\,410~m$^3$/ha reported for grain maize in Occitanie in 2021 \parencite{agreste2026occitanie}. A plausibility rule fixed in advance (500 to 3\,000~m$^3$/ha) excludes \WatExcl{} of the \WatN{} department-years. Dividing the observed yield gap (median \WatGain~q/ha) by the dose gives about \WatKg~kg of grain and \WatEur{} euros of gross production per cubic metre (\WatEurTen{} with the 2010 shares; from \WatEurMin{} to \WatEurMax{} across departments). For comparison, a study of irrigating maize farms in the Adour-Garonne basin estimates the margin attributable to irrigation water at 0.20 to 0.28 euros per cubic metre in 2012--2014, before depreciation of equipment, for 1.4~kg of maize per cubic metre \parencite{draaf2017}. A gross value should exceed a margin, and it does. The figure is descriptive: the observed gap also reflects differences between irrigated and rainfed land, and the dose assumes that maize receives as much water per hectare as the other irrigated crops of the department. In hot summers the dose rises (\WatDoseSlope~m$^3$/ha per degree-day, $p = \WatDoseSlopeP$), but the gap per hectare does not rise detectably in these six departments ($p = \WatGainSlopeP$), so the value of the extra water of hot summers cannot be measured this way.

\subsection{Summary}

In the main sample, summer heat is associated with a wider yield advantage of irrigated over rainfed maize. The association satisfies the rule fixed before any estimation on 2000--2009, although \ExploSharePct\,\% of its sample comes from the years that suggested it; keeps its sign, with weak evidence, on a decade the exploratory analysis had not used; survives an older parcel mask, other temperature sources and flexible weather controls; and replicates on silage maize. Heat of the following summer, which cannot affect the harvest, is unrelated to the gap. Its magnitude is uncertain and rests largely on 2003. It is not significant with a cluster jackknife ($p = \CvThreeP$), nor without the six departments with uplands, although their slope does not differ detectably from that of the others. It depends on measuring heat where maize is grown, a choice made in advance and supported by the geography of the departments concerned, but one that matters. It is an association, not the causal effect of irrigation.
